Does it have to be typographically distinct?

Distinct inside the genre's signals, yes. Fans read "bass" from a few cues: heavy weight, capitals, hand-built letters, a mascot beside the word. Stay inside those cues and FANGSPEARE reads as bass at a glance. Then own one detail nobody else has, and that detail is what makes it memorable. The rune S, a hat-peak A, two ember eyes: any one of them can be yours.

Literary plus dubstep can live in layers. The dubstep lives in the mark's weight and silhouette. The literary lives in the titles, the liner notes and the sage's story, where a Mincho serif sings.

Five recommendations

  1. Lead with the Icon. One line weight and two ember eyes. It reads at avatar size, and it's already unlike anything in the survey.
  2. Pick one wordmark to travel with it, and repeat the pair everywhere: Icon left of the word, as Dirt Monkey and Mersiv do, or stacked above it, as Marshmello does.
  3. The jersey letters are a strong candidate. No act in the survey uses runes, and heavy condensed caps read best at distance. If they start from Norse Bold, finish them as outlined custom artwork; the font's license covers logos.
  4. Let the site header's mazes and spirals become the pattern system: trims, sleeves, LED loops and limited drops, so your second design shows up everywhere without competing.
  5. Give Shippori the literary job: release titles, track lists and liner notes. The Seal handles stamps, stickers and seals of approval.

The jersey mark

What we see: runic letters, a navy-on-ice palette with a jade collar, a crackle like frozen water across the body, and the frog Seal pressed on like wax. It already looks like a club kit from a myth.

  1. Where did the runic letters come from? Did you draw them, or start from a font and break it?

The site header

What we see: wide spacing that reads from far away, and patterns carved into every letter, so it rewards a close look too.

  1. The mazes and spirals inside the letters: what are they to you?

The frog sage

What we see: a sage drawn from two living traditions. There are the toad heroes of Japanese woodblock prints, and the didgeridoo, which comes from Arnhem Land in Aboriginal Australia, where the Yolŋu call it the yidaki.

  1. The sage draws on a printmaking tradition with real names behind it. What would you want to give back to it: a drop that credits the print that inspired it, or a merch note naming Kuniyoshi?
