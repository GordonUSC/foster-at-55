FANGSPEAREThe Descent

Your private studio for ancient alien dubstep. Four levels, twelve sessions, and an optional story path inspired by The Mound. Study Hamdi’s performances, then make choices that sound and feel like you.

Headphones, the study videos, and a notebook are enough to start listening and counting. Cueing and mixing drills need a two-deck DJ setup or equivalent software; return to those when you have access.

Begin the descent

Each level gives you a private practice piece and an optional episode plan. Use the story prompts, change them, or build your own. Sharing and reaching listeners are later choices; nothing posts on its own.

01 The Grid 35 min

Find the one

Hear beats, bars and phrase changes without needing a track ID.

Study tape Niteharts Festival ↗

Watch for: Find a steady passage and count eight bars.

  1. Choose one track you own with a steady 4/4 passage.
  2. Count eight bars aloud and tap only beat one.
  3. Record five short attempts. Check each return against the track’s actual accents or a verified grid.
  4. Circle the attempt where you lost the count; write where you restarted.
  1. Choose an unfamiliar 4/4 track with clear sections.
  2. Before the next change, say “new section here” on the downbeat you predict.
  3. Check five predictions. Separate a missed downbeat from a wrong phrase-length assumption.
  4. Repeat with a half-time-feeling track and explain two possible BPM counts.

Pass mark Four of five attempts retain the bar count through eight bars. If the source has an irregular phrase, explain it instead of forcing a grid.

Pass mark At least three of five predictions land on the actual section boundary; explain each miss musically.

Counting
  1. BeginningNeeds restarting within eight bars.
  2. SecureKeeps eight bars accurately in four of five attempts.
  3. AdaptableMaintains count through sparse drums and explains a phrase exception.
Evidence
  1. BeginningNames a technique without an audible event.
  2. SecureNames the element that changed and the time.
  3. AdaptableSeparates observation, explanation and uncertainty.
Go deeper

First, tap the steady pulse. In these 4/4 exercises, count “one, two, three, four.” On each new bar, change only the first number: “two, two, three, four,” then “three, two, three, four.” After eight bars, listen for a change, but keep counting if no change happens. The arrangement decides where the sentence ends.

A kick is a low drum sound; a snare or clap is a sharper accent. In half-time-feeling bass music, the spacious backbeat can make 140 BPM feel like 70. Neither a sparse drum pattern nor a software BPM label tells you the entire phrasing. Count the music, then test the count against a return, fill or new element.

Getting lost is useful information. Go back four bars, listen for a stronger anchor, and restart. You are training a dependable internal map, not proving you already know every style.

While you watch

  • Find a steady passage and count eight bars.
  • Mark three changes that sound like new sentences. What actually changes: drums, bass, vocal, texture or silence?

If it gets stuck

  • If The snare feels like beat one every time. Then Use a simple kick-led passage, count four beats aloud, then bring that count back to the harder track.
  • If You count correctly but predict the wrong section. Then Map the arrangement. Timing and phrase-length prediction are different skills.

02 The Grid 35 min

Hear the right thing

Understand cueing, channel level and the audience output before adding complexity.

Study tape Niteharts Festival ↗

Watch for: Describe one clear change in musical weight without assuming it was a volume change.

Checked moments B2 Visible contact does not name the control

  1. At a comfortable monitoring level, play A to the master.
  2. Keep B out of the audience mix and audition it using the setup’s cue path. Verify the routing.
  3. Listen to a loud section of B, establish sensible channel gain and return to its start cue.
  4. Blend B in and out three times, checking channel and master meters.
  5. Replay a recording if your setup supports it.
  1. Pick two tracks that sound different in level or density.
  2. Match them by listening and metering, not identical knob positions.
  3. Record three transitions at one unchanged master setting.
  4. Describe whether differences are loudness, density, bass weight or all three.

Pass mark Three preparations with no unintended cue audio in the master, no visible clipping and no startling unintended level jump.

Pass mark Three usable transitions without chasing the master level; identify where upstream gain correction was needed.

Routing
  1. BeginningCannot yet identify what reaches headphones versus master.
  2. SecurePrepares the incoming track privately and identifies the live channel.
  3. AdaptableCan diagnose an unexpected monitoring path without disturbing the live track.
Level control
  1. BeginningCorrects everything with the master knob.
  2. SecureChecks source and summed levels with no observed clipping.
  3. AdaptableBalances contrasting tracks and distinguishes density from loudness.
Go deeper

Headphone cue lets you prepare a source without necessarily sending it to the audience. Master is the audience mix. Before practicing, identify the playing deck, its mixer channel, the incoming deck, the headphone selection and the master path. Do this slowly. A button labeled CUE on a player may return to a track position; a mixer CUE button may select monitoring.

Use trim or gain to establish an appropriate channel level, then use faders for the musical blend. Watch the relevant meters and follow your equipment’s normal operating guidance; there is no universal “correct clock position” or meter color across every mixer. Check a loud section before the transition. Combining tracks, EQ boosts and effects can use additional headroom.

If a channel is clipping, reduce the level at that stage. Turning the final master down can make clipped audio quieter but cannot restore the original signal. Do not demonstrate clipping at high volume. Meter level and perceived energy are different things.

While you watch

  • Describe one clear change in musical weight without assuming it was a volume change.
  • If hands or controls are obscured, leave the control name unknown.

If it gets stuck

  • If The recording gets loud only during the overlap. Then Reduce overlapping material or channel level. Recheck EQ boosts and effects before touching the master.
  • If You hear B but the recording does not. Then Check whether you were hearing cue rather than master. This may be correct routing, not a fault.

03 The Grid 35 min

Lock the groove

Diagnose offset versus drift and keep a short overlap stable.

Study tape Niteharts Festival ↗

Watch for: Find an overlap if you can hear one; note whether the groove feels continuous.

  1. In practice, prepare two steady tracks at the same intended tempo.
  2. Align their beats and hold a 16-bar overlap with simple percussion.
  3. Repeat three times. If they separate, decide whether the error stays constant or grows.
  4. Use the appropriate small correction and record the result. Sync is allowed; note whether it was used.
  1. Use two tracks with verified steady grids.
  2. Hide stacked waveforms while keeping essential level information visible, or turn attention away from the beat display.
  3. Create a small timing offset in the incoming, non-live deck and correct it by ear.
  4. Hold a 32-bar overlap and recover from one small planned offset without stopping the outgoing track.

Pass mark Three 16-bar overlaps with no persistent audible doubled beat; each correction is labeled offset, drift or unsure.

Pass mark A 32-bar overlap plus one controlled recovery. Track the approximate bars needed to recover; aim to shorten your own baseline rather than chase a universal time.

Diagnosis
  1. BeginningChanges tempo for every audible mismatch.
  2. SecureDistinguishes constant offset from growing drift.
  3. AdaptableRecognizes a grid or source-tempo problem and chooses a safe alternative.
Stability
  1. BeginningLoses the groove during overlap.
  2. SecureHolds sixteen bars in three attempts.
  3. AdaptableHolds thirty-two bars and recovers from a small offset.
Go deeper

Two problems can sound similar. If beats are consistently displaced by the same amount, their positions are offset. If they move farther apart over time, their speeds differ or one track has changing tempo. A temporary nudge changes position; a tempo adjustment changes the rate of future drift. Make small changes and listen again.

Sync is a legitimate tool. It depends on what the software actually synchronizes and on an accurate musical map. A wrong first beat or grid can produce a confidently wrong result. Use the screen as a reference and your ears as the check. Learn to recognize the failure even if you choose to use Sync in performance.

Start with two simple, steady rhythmic passages at compatible tempos. A difficult polyrhythm, busy vocal or shifting live drummer can wait. Your first target is a stable overlap, not an impressive number of simultaneous decks.

While you watch

  • Find an overlap if you can hear one; note whether the groove feels continuous.
  • Do not claim manual beatmatching or Sync from the finished sound alone.

If it gets stuck

  • If The tracks repeatedly slide after a nudge. Then Check tempo and the source’s stability; another nudge alone may only postpone the same problem.
  • If The screen says matched but it sounds wrong. Then Check the first downbeat and grid against the audio. Reduce to a simpler passage.

04 Your Set 35 min

Know what each track can do

Build a small, useful crate and a trustworthy map of each track.

Study tape Niteharts Festival ↗

Watch for: Choose one passage where the new musical idea becomes clear.

  1. Choose six available tracks, not six new purchases.
  2. For each, write three sound descriptors and one possible set role.
  3. Mark a safe starting point and exit; count the distance to one major arrival.
  4. If you have cue support, save these markers and verify them by replaying. Otherwise keep a paper timestamp map.
  1. Arrange the same six tracks for a gradual opening.
  2. Rearrange them for a shorter, high-contrast performance.
  3. Explain three changed adjacencies: why this track after that track?
  4. Prepare a calmer and a more intense next-track option for one point.

Pass mark All six tracks have a verified entry, exit and one counted section; at least two different musical roles appear.

Pass mark Two defensible routes using the same music, with three specific sequencing explanations and two fallback choices.

Preparation
  1. BeginningMarkers are guessed or incomplete.
  2. SecureSix tracks have checked entries, exits and warnings.
  3. AdaptableCan explain and change a route while keeping useful exits.
Selection language
  1. BeginningUses only genre or “good/bad.”
  2. SecureNames audible texture and a musical role.
  3. AdaptableExplains why a role changes with its surrounding tracks.
Go deeper

Begin with six tracks you genuinely like. Give each a job: establish a groove, add tension, release, make space, change direction or close. A track can do more than one job depending on where you start and what precedes it. Keep descriptive tags specific: “dry percussion, short vocal, wide bass gaps” tells you more than “banger.”

A cue is a navigation aid. Mark the first useful phrase, a preparation point before the section you want, a major arrival, a reliable exit and an optional safe loop. Label the preparation point by its actual distance, such as “16 bars to main section.” Cue colors are your convention, not a universal standard. Check your software’s behavior before relying on it.

Listen through the whole track at least once. Write down surprise vocals, odd-length sections, tempo changes or an ending that cuts sooner than expected. The best library is one you can find your way around under pressure.

While you watch

  • Choose one passage where the new musical idea becomes clear.
  • Describe what made it useful at that point, without requiring an exact track ID.

If it gets stuck

  • If Everything is tagged peak. Then Listen at a fixed comfortable level and compare percussion density, bass space and vocal activity; assign at least one breathing-space role.

05 Your Set 35 min

Make room for the bass

Transfer musical weight deliberately without a muddy or empty surprise.

Study tape Boiler Room Bristol ↗

Watch for: Find one moment where low-end weight appears to move or change.

Checked moments B2 Visible contact does not name the control

  1. Use a steady compatible pair with uncluttered phrases.
  2. Start a 16-bar overlap with B’s low end reduced and A carrying the bass.
  3. Choose the next clear phrase boundary for the handoff. Transfer bass focus and then exit A.
  4. Repeat three times at the same comfortable master level.
  5. Replay the handoff and mark muddy overlap, unintended hole, level jump or clean/intentional change.
  1. Record a continuous-weight handoff.
  2. Record a brief, intentional thinning before B arrives.
  3. Record a sharper cut at a clean boundary.
  4. Compare the three at similar listening level and choose which best serves your imagined set chapter.

Pass mark Three handoffs with no unplanned bass collision or prolonged unplanned gap; the target boundary is named before each take.

Pass mark Three recognizably different results with a reasoned choice; no version earns extra credit merely for being louder.

Low-end space
  1. BeginningBoth bass parts overlap accidentally or disappear unexpectedly.
  2. SecureOne dominant bass role transfers on the intended boundary.
  3. AdaptableUses continuity or temporary absence intentionally and can explain the tradeoff.
Listening
  1. BeginningAssumes knob movement guarantees the result.
  2. SecureChecks the recorded sound as well as levels.
  3. AdaptableAdjusts for tracks whose important sounds span multiple EQ regions.
Go deeper

EQ changes frequency regions. In bass-heavy music, two full low ends can compete, add excessive level or partially cancel, depending on the material. For this beginner drill, let one track own the main bass role at a time. This is a learning constraint; experienced blends can deliberately break it.

At a matched tempo, introduce B’s useful higher-frequency material with its low end reduced. At a chosen phrase boundary, transfer the low-frequency focus from A to B. Then remove what A no longer contributes. Listen to the result. An EQ may not fully remove a band, and a sound can cross multiple bands; a knob position alone does not prove the mix is clean.

A good handoff preserves an intentional groove and weight. Sometimes a brief planned thinning is exactly the point. Decide beforehand whether you want continuity or contrast, then judge that decision rather than rewarding the busiest movement.

While you watch

  • Find one moment where low-end weight appears to move or change.
  • Separate the audible change from an unverified EQ, filter, edit or arrangement explanation.

If it gets stuck

  • If The blend is muddy although one low EQ is down. Then Try a less dense section, shorter overlap or additional careful level/EQ adjustment. “Low” does not isolate every bass-related sound.
  • If The handoff feels weak. Then Check whether both tracks are attenuated at once, whether the incoming arrangement is sparse, and whether a clean cut is musically better.

06 Your Set 40 min

Choose the right exit

Use a blend, a cut and a loop for distinct musical purposes.

Study tape Boiler Room Bristol ↗

Watch for: Locate one change that sounds gradual and one that sounds sudden, if both are present.

Checked moments B2 Visible contact does not name the control

  1. Prepare one compatible pair with clear sections.
  2. Record a planned sixteen-bar phrase blend.
  3. Record a clean boundary cut.
  4. Practice a tested loop on A while cueing B, then leave the loop at your counted boundary.
  5. Score clarity, timing and musical fit for each exit.
  1. Prepare three short scenarios: a vocal is about to clash; the incoming track is not ready; the outgoing phrase is ending sooner than expected.
  2. Choose an exit method before touching the controls.
  3. Perform each scenario and explain the rejected alternative.
  4. Optional only after clean basics: test a short, low-feedback, tempo-checked echo tail on your own setup and confirm how it stops.

Pass mark One usable example of each exit and a correct account of its purpose. No unplanned stop; no uncontrolled repeated loop.

Pass mark Three intentional responses with safe exits and one rejected option explained per scenario. Effects are not required.

Phrase alignment
  1. BeginningCounts bars but ignores where the target sections arrive.
  2. SecureLaunches B early enough for the intended sections to meet.
  3. AdaptableChanges overlap length when the arrangement requires it.
Exit choice
  1. BeginningUses one exit regardless of material.
  2. SecureCan execute a blend, cut and loop exit.
  3. AdaptableChooses a simpler method when it better protects the music.
Go deeper

A long transition is not automatically better. A blend can preserve continuity; a cut can make contrast clear; a loop can hold a stable section while you prepare. The transition should suit the material and the story. Avoid stacking two competing lead vocals just because their beats line up.

For a phrase blend, count backward from the desired arrival. If B’s target section is sixteen bars after its cue, launch that cue sixteen bars before the chosen meeting point in A. Verify the actual arrangement. You can plan a shorter overlap if either track changes unexpectedly.

For a cut, choose an intentional boundary and move the musical focus decisively without an accidental gap or double hit. For a safety loop, first test a stable four- or eight-bar passage and how to exit it. Grid-based loop lengths and quantized actions depend on accurate settings. Effects are optional; add them only after the unprocessed exit works.

While you watch

  • Locate one change that sounds gradual and one that sounds sudden, if both are present.
  • Describe why each works or does not work for you; avoid guessing the exact fader or effect.

If it gets stuck

  • If The two drops miss each other. Then Count the actual bars from B’s cue to its arrival, then work backward from A’s intended boundary.
  • If The loop helps but you cannot leave it cleanly. Then Practice loop exit alone at low complexity before adding another track.

07 The Room 40 min

Connect different musical worlds

Make track choices using rhythm, texture and purpose rather than BPM alone.

Study tape Boiler Room Bristol ↗

Watch for: Choose two adjacent musical passages that feel different.

  1. From your six-track crate, choose three pairs.
  2. For each, name one shared feature, one contrast and one intended audience feeling.
  3. Audition the overlap at a comfortable level.
  4. Write “works,” “needs a shorter overlap,” or “choose another track,” with an audible reason.
  1. Choose a start and destination that feel meaningfully different.
  2. Find an intermediate track or a suitable reset section.
  3. Rehearse a two-step bridge and a simpler direct alternative.
  4. Compare which better serves the moment; do not assume the longer route is superior.

Pass mark Three specific selection explanations and at least one honest rejected or revised pairing.

Pass mark Two viable routes, a justified preference and a safe fallback exit.

Compatibility
  1. BeginningChooses by BPM or key label alone.
  2. SecureNames audible reasons a pairing works or clashes.
  3. AdaptableBuilds a bridge while retaining a simple alternative.
Taste
  1. BeginningCopies a reference sequence without a reason.
  2. SecureChooses music genuinely connected to Fangspeare’s taste.
  3. AdaptableExplains both the influence and the deliberate departure.
Go deeper

Compare five features before a mix: rhythmic feel, percussion density, bass character, vocal or melodic focus, and emotional function. Two tracks at the same BPM can feel incompatible. Two different styles may connect through a shared drum texture, a vocal idea or a convincing moment of space.

A 70/140 relationship can describe compatible pulse levels, but the arrangement and accents still have to work. Do not assume every pair with a doubled number will blend. When tempi are genuinely incompatible, a deliberate reset at a breakdown or a clean contrasting cut may be more honest than forcing a long overlap.

Key labels can suggest candidate combinations; your ears decide whether the actual overlapping notes work. Sparse percussion may leave more room than two prominent melodies. If a transition clashes, shorten the overlap, choose a less melodic section or choose a different next track. Harmonic compatibility is a tool, not the whole artistic brief.

While you watch

  • Choose two adjacent musical passages that feel different.

A signature moment begins with a musical reason. You might briefly introduce a motif, remove it, and bring it back when the surrounding rhythm gives it a different meaning. The lexicon

Plain words for what you hear. Open one when you need it.

Beat / pulse

The regular time reference you tap along to. It is not necessarily every drum hit.

BPM

Beats per minute. A speed label, not an energy score. Half-time and double-time counting can describe the same audio at different numbers.

Bar

A repeating group of beats. Our beginner drills use four beats per bar; music can use other meters.

Downbeat

Beat one of a bar. The first downbeat of a new section is especially useful for a planned mix.

Phrase

A musical sentence that groups bars. Eight, sixteen and thirty-two bars are useful places to investigate in dance music, not laws every track follows.

Arrangement

The order of a track’s sections and the way elements enter or leave over time.

Deck / channel

A deck plays a track; its mixer channel controls how that source contributes to the output. Their numbering and routing depend on the setup.

Cue

Either a saved/start position in a track, or a headphone-monitoring selection. These are different jobs even when both buttons say CUE.

Beatgrid

The software’s map of beat locations. A neat-looking grid can still be wrong; check it against the music.

Beatmatch

Make two rhythmic sources run at compatible speeds with their intended beats aligned.

Phase / alignment

Where one beat falls relative to the other. Two tracks can have the same BPM while their beats are offset.

Gain / trim

Input-level adjustment used to establish a workable channel level. It is not a substitute for a planned musical energy change.

Headroom

Space before a signal reaches the system’s limit. Leave room for louder moments and overlapping tracks.

Clipping

A signal exceeds a stage’s available range and is distorted. Lowering a later master control cannot undo clipping that already happened upstream.

EQ

Controls that change frequency regions, often low, middle and high. Their ranges and maximum cuts differ across mixers.

Bass handoff

A deliberate transfer of the low-frequency focus from one track to another. One dominant bass source is a helpful beginner constraint, not a universal law.

Filter

A frequency-shaping control that can progressively remove part of the sound. Its response, resonance and neutral setting depend on the equipment.

Loop

A selected passage repeated until you leave it. A loop can buy preparation time or reshape a musical idea.

Quantize

A feature that aligns supported actions to a beatgrid. Supported actions and resolution depend on the product; a wrong grid can produce a wrong result.

Drop / release

A conspicuous arrival or return of musical weight after preparation. A strong release is created by context, not just more volume.

Tension / space

Tension is the feeling of anticipation; space is room left by fewer or less dominant elements. Both are listening judgments, not meter readings.

Energy

Your contextual impression of intensity, shaped by rhythm, density, bass, texture, familiarity and contrast as well as level.
